Abstract—Network trafﬁc classiﬁcation stands as an essential
endeavor within the realms of network security and manage-
ment. The recent advances in learning-based methodologies
have underscored their efﬁcacy in deducing patterns from
the side-channel features of encrypted network trafﬁc. The
unpredictability of trafﬁc bursts can result in packet loss
during retransmission, thereby generating fragmented feature
patterns. Unfortunately, current approaches struggle to adapt
to such fragmented features, often leading to a substantial
decline in performance. To surmount this challenge, this paper
introduces a pre-training-based framework, denoted as N ¨uwa,
which imputes the side-channel features of encrypted network
trafﬁc, especially focusing on the temporal attributes of missing
packets within a trafﬁc session. Firstly, we propose a word-level
Sequence2Embedding (S2E) module to transform side-channel
features into tokens for model pre-training, as well as a Trafﬁc
Feature Masking strategy (TFM) to simulate the original ﬂows
changes in packet loss network. Besides, we also introduce a
Trafﬁc Feature Imputation (TFI) module to restore the missing
values of original trafﬁc ﬂows in an efﬁcient and context-aware
manner. Experiments across four diverse real-world scenarios
substantiate N ¨uwa’s capacity to restore the performance of
prevalent temporal models, while maintaining the integrity of
the imputed features. Notably, N ¨uwa has also demonstrated an
impressive resilience, even under conditions of extensive feature
loss and domain adaptation. The N ¨uwa prototype has been made
accessible to the public for further research and development
(https://github.com/Timeless-zfqi/Nuwa).
Index Terms —Trafﬁc packet loss, side-channel feature, pre-
training, feature imputation, domain adaption.

completeness of raw trafﬁc data, with missing data handled
through simple ﬁlling strategies (e.g., zero-padding or mean
imputation). However, the escalating complexity of network
environments and the information loss caused by packet drops
pose signiﬁcant challenges to trafﬁc classiﬁcation tasks [2].
With the surge in encrypted trafﬁc, traditional rule-based
methods [3] have become increasingly limited, which only
focus on payload information.
Currently, the primary approach to NTAC tasks involves
extracting side-channel features from raw trafﬁc, categorized
as feature engineering based methods. Despite their great per-
formances, we have to ﬁgure out that they are inadequate for
practical application, particularly in scenarios involving packet
loss within network trafﬁc, like wireless networks, subways,
and etc. In other words, in practical network environments,
these methods in the trafﬁc domain fail to effectively address
packet loss issues in trafﬁc data. Therefore, how to effectively
restoring the side-channel features of raw data with missing
packets is crucial for NTAC tasks.
Network trafﬁc side-channel feature imputation, aiming
to recover missing packet information in trafﬁc data, is
an important technique in optimizing network management
and bolstering security measures [4]. Traditional imputation
methods for missing side-channel features basically rely on
observed data, such as averages or nearest neighbor values, and
techniques like Markov model [5]. However, these methods
do not fully leverage session-wide information, thus suffering
from the long-distance forgetting. These heuristic methods
overlook the negative impacts of imputation on downstream
tasks. Furthermore, in other ﬁelds, such as trafﬁc data and
image processing, researchers have proposed various advanced
imputation methods to handle missing data. For instance, deep
learning techniques like SAGCRN [6] can ﬁll in missing parts
by learning the spatiotemporal dependencies of the data. While
imputation methods from non-trafﬁc domains demonstrate
excellent performance, they cannot be directly applied to
restore missing trafﬁc-related information due to the inevitable
gaps between these tasks.

large unlabeled datasets to learn unbiased logical relationships

like ET-BERT [7] and MAE [8] apply pre-trained models to

Speciﬁcally, the proposed N ¨uwa is able to address random
packet loss, consecutive packet loss, and out-of-order packets
through the TFM masking strategy. With these pre-training
strategies, N ¨uwa only needs to be trained on large-scale
unlabeled raw data. Such a new network trafﬁc side-channel
feature imputation methods is practical and feasible for in-deep
network measurement.

Assumptions. In designing and evaluating the trafﬁc feature
imputation model based on the TFM strategy, we make the fol-
lowing assumptions to clearly deﬁne the scope of the research
and the boundary conditions of the application scenarios:
Assumptions on Data Loss Characteristics. i) Packet loss
typically exhibits local characteristics. Missing data points in
sequences are not entirely random but tend to occur within
speciﬁc time intervals or follow certain patterns (e.g., bursty
packet loss). ii) Even with data loss, parts of the trafﬁc
sequence remain intact. The imputation model can utilize
contextual information from these intact segments to infer and
recover the missing data.
Assumptions on Noise Distribution. The TFM strategy
generates mask data (e.g., random, Poisson-distributed, or
locally concentrated missing positions) to approximate the
characteristics of data loss observed in real-world networks.
This assumption ensures that the masking strategies employed
during the pre-training phase are aligned with realistic scenar-
ios, allowing the imputation model to generalize effectively to
real-world applications. Moreover, the noise mask distribution
does not intentionally favor speciﬁc trafﬁc patterns, thereby
avoiding the introduction of biased assumptions during train-
ing and preserving the model’s generalizability.
Assumptions on Trafﬁc Feature Reliability. Despite packet
loss, the quality of the remaining trafﬁc feature values are
reliable and have not been subject to additional tampering
or malicious interference (such as data pollution or attacks).
The pre-training task using the noise-based masking strategy
(Masked Language Modeling, MLM) is consistent with the
real-world trafﬁc feature imputation task in terms of data dis-
tribution and missing patterns. Therefore, the representations
learned by the imputation model during the pre-training phase
can be effectively transferred to real-world imputation tasks.

III. M EASUREMENT STUDY
In this section, to better illustrate the effects of trafﬁc
side-channel feature loss on existing models, we systemat-
ically evaluate the performance of six representative deep
learning models on real-world and simulated network trafﬁc
with packet loss. The models in this paper employs tra-
ditional Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Long-Short
Term Memory (LSTM), GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit) [3],
Denoising Autoencoder (SDAE) [10]algorithm, and advanced
Time Series Transformer (TST) [11], FS-Net [1] algorithm for
validation. We conducted experiments in two environments:
real network packet loss and simulated packet loss, and we
apply two metrics, namely accuracy (Acc) and F1-Score (F1)
to evaluate the trafﬁc classiﬁcation results.
We conducted experiments across various real-world net-
work trafﬁc scenarios, including website trafﬁc via DoH (DNS
over HTTPS), IoT trafﬁc for small devices, mobile application
trafﬁc for mobile devices, and PC terminal trafﬁc.
Scenario d1 involves trafﬁc generated by mobile applica-
tions. We selected the CIC-AndAdw-2017 dataset [12], which
was obtained by installing Android applications on real devices
and capturing their network trafﬁc. The dataset is generated
from 1,900 applications with the following three categories:
Adware (250 apps, 13345 sessions), General Malware (150
apps, 8673 sessions) and Benign (1500 apps, 29710 sessions).
Specially, the network trafﬁc of both the malware and benign
(20% malware and 80% benign).
Scenario d2 is website DoH network trafﬁc, using the
CIRA-CIC-DoHBrw-2020 dataset [13], which provides for
analyzing, testing, and evaluating DoH trafﬁc within covert
channels and tunnels. DoH was obtained by accessing the top
10k Alexa websites, using browsers and DNS tunneling tools
that support the DoH protocol. It contains Non-DoH (brows-
ing thousands of websites from Alexa domain), Benign-DoH
(using Mozilla Firefox and Google Chrome web browsers)
and Malicious-DoH (DNS2TCP, DNScat2, Iodine, generated
by DNS tunnel tools) with 193,883 sessions.
Scenariod3 is a real-time dataset and benchmark for large-
scale attacks in IoT environment [14], using an extensive
topology compose of 105 real IoT devices and adopting
IoT devices as attackers and victims. It’s divided into 7
classes against IoT devices and demonstrated how they
can be reproduced. In this study, we focus on six types
of TCP-based attacks, including DoS-HTTP-Flood, DDoS-
HTTP-Flood, DoS-TCP-Flood, MITM-Spooﬁng, DDoS-TCP-

A. Experiments With Simulated Trafﬁc Packet Loss
1) Experiment Setup: Simulating Trafﬁc Packet Loss. To
emulate real-world conditions in our testbed, we introduce
packet loss using both random and Poisson distributions,
removing 10%, 20%, and 30% of packets from the trafﬁc. In
different network segments and environments, the packet loss
rate varies. In typical cases, network packet loss is below 10%,
while in MAN, the packet loss rate can range from 10% to
20% [16]. To account for extreme conditions, we also set the
packet loss rate to 30% in our experiments to better perform
stress testing. The raw trafﬁc is pre-processed to isolate TCP
ﬂows, which are then segmented into individual sessions. For
each session, bi-directional packet length features are extracted
to serve as the basis for analysis.
Original Flow Data Processing. We cap the maximum
packet length at 1600 bytes. This decision is based on the
Ethernet protocol (RFC 894), which is widely used for trans-
mission and deﬁnes the maximum transmission unit (MTU) as
1500 bytes, including both the payload and the packet header.
However, considering the variability introduced by local area
network (LAN) conﬁgurations and potential MTU adjustments
during internal and external network transitions, we extend the
threshold slightly to accommodate such variations, setting the
maximum packet length to 1600 bytes. Furthermore, based on
practical considerations and the handshake and retransmission
mechanisms inherent to TCP, we deﬁne the minimum session
length as 20 packets. To simulate real-world network condi-
tions, we introduce random packet loss by applying masks
ranging from 10% to 30%.

across environments d1, d2, d3, and d4, we adhere to an
8:2 split for training and testing ﬂows. Subsequently, we

IV. S YSTEM DESIGN OF N ¨UWA
Real-world network are dynamic and unpredictable, with
factors like trafﬁc bursts causing signiﬁcant variability. We
require restore the original side-channel features to reduce the
impact of packet loss on existing DL models by N ¨uwa.
A. Overview
We propose a network trafﬁc side-channel feature imputa-
tion framework, termed N ¨uwa, which employs a pre-trained
noise masking approach. N ¨uwa utilizes a Transformer-based
neural network architecture comprising three modules: a
Sequence2Embedding (S2E) for trafﬁc representation, a Traf-
ﬁc Feature Masking strategy (TFM), and a Trafﬁc Feature
Imputation model (TFI). The framework’s architecture is
depicted in Fig. 5. The ﬁrst module is used to transform side-
channel features into tokens for pre-training. TFM can help us
to obtain a number of new ﬂows that simulate how the original
ﬂows change in packet loss network. And the missing values
are imputed by TFI to restore the original ﬂows.
N¨uwa aims to maintain the performance of DL models in
real network with packet loss by learning a generic representa-
tion of encrypted trafﬁc’s side-channel features and restoring
them across various scenarios, including Website, IoT, Mobile
Application, and PC Terminal trafﬁc. Without requiring trafﬁc
labels for speciﬁc scenarios or prior network knowledge, the
N¨uwa framework consists of two stages: (1) pre-training,
where TFM strategy is applied to learn application side-
channel feature patterns from a large unlabeled dataset, and
(2) feature imputation, where imputation model predicts and
ﬁlls in missing values within input sequences, enabling the
restoration of intact features for downstream tasks.
B. Sequence2Embedding Module
In the realm of NLP, pre-trained language models such as
RoBERTa [18] and DeBERTa [19] commonly employ the Byte
Pair Encoding (BPE) [18] method for sentence tokenization.
However, for trafﬁc side-channel features, the BPE’s fragments
often lack semantic meaning. For example, the sequence s =
[52, -44, 40, ... , -1500, 40] tokenized by BPE results in
s1 = [52, 44, 40, ... , 00] and combined sub-words s2 =
[5200, 521, 441, ... , 5215]. This paper introduces: (1) a
WordPiece token generator for encoding packet length and
timestamp sequences, and (2) a Token2Embedding that uses
content and position embeddings to enhance temporal and
content relationships in input sequences.
WordPiece token generator. We introduce a word-level
token generator applicable to a variety of features. For the
packet length feature, each value is treated as a distinct word
within the range (-1600, 1600). We scale the values of the
trafﬁc packet length features to the interval [0, 3200] and
derive the vocabulary from the raw data. As depicted in Fig. 5
Phase I, for the packet arrival time feature, timestamps are
ampliﬁed by a factor of 1e4 and then truncated to integers to
establish a timestamp vocabulary with a range of [0, 10000].
Token2Embedding. Trafﬁc sequence features are uniquely
deﬁned by packet direction, size, and arrival time. The relative
positioning of packets within ﬂows and sessions is ﬁxed. For
example, the TCP protocol’s Three-way Handshake and Four-
Way Wavehand are sequential patterns. Moreover, application
trafﬁc features may show periodicity, such as in the website
sequence [52, -52, 40, 457, -40, -1500, -1500, 40, -1500,
-1500, 40, -1500, -1500, -1500, -1500, 40, ... , -40, 40, 40,
-40]. Here, both the initial [40, -1500, -1500] 1 and the later
[40, -1500, -1500]2 are contextually similar, but have different
arrival times, indicating that the absolute position of a masked
word in the packet sequence is signiﬁcant.
Each token from S2E is represented by two embeddings:
content and position. Unlike BERT [7] and ET-BERT [20],
which sum different embedding types for the ﬁnal represen-
tation, we omit SEP and NSP, treating the sequence as an
intact sentence. We introduce special tokens [PAD], [s], [/s],
[UNK], and [MASK] with our WordPiece token generator .
Each input sequence is standardized to 514 tokens, initiated
by [s], terminated by [/s], and padded to 512 feature tokens
using [PAD]. The embedding for each token, SIi,j, jointly

content and position, and attention weights are calculated using

C. Trafﬁc Noising Based Self-Supervised Pre-Training Mask
Strategy
The pre-training model, akin to BERT, employs a mask
strategy where 10% of the mask tokens remain unchanged,
10% are substituted with random tokens, and 80% are replaced
by [MASK] tokens. RoBERTa and DeBERTa adopt this
approach but also implement a dynamic masking strategy
where the masked words within a sentence change with each
iteration.
We contend that a dynamic mask strategy alone is insuf-
ﬁcient to capture the true characteristics of network trafﬁc
packet transmission, which includes the loss of side-channel
features due to packet loss, as well as potential delays and
out-of-order issues at each network node. In this paper, we
introduce the TFM strategy, as illustrated in Fig. 5 Phase II
TFM, encompassing Token Mask, Token Deletion, Span Mask,
Random Replace, and Unchanged.
Token Mask. In instances of discrete packet loss, where
the sequence exhibits non-continuous gaps, a random selection
of tokens from the original sequence is replaced with the
special [MASK] token. These masked tokens are subsequently
predicted and imputed by the TFI.
Token Deletion. Tokens are randomly removed from the
input sequence, which may be consecutive or non-consecutive.
Span Mask. In scenarios where network disruptions lead to
the loss of multiple consecutive packets, we replace multiple
consecutive sub-sequences within the original sequence with a
unique [MASK] token. Each continuous sub-sequence follows
a Poisson distribution with λ = 3 . This masking strategy is
inspired by SpanBERT, which samples text fragment lengths
based on a geometric distribution and replaces these fragments
with sequences of [MASK] tokens of the same length. Unlike
SpanBERT, our approach involves multiple samplings from
a Poisson distribution to represent sub-sequences of varying
lengths, allowing the model to learn the quantity of missing
tokens in a trafﬁc sequence through Span Mask.
Random Replace. To address the packet arrival out-of-
order issue, two positions within the sequence are randomly
selected and their values are swapped.
Unchanged. The position subjected to random masking
retains its original value unaltered.
Therefore, the pre-training loss L of the TFI is:
L =LMask +LSpan +LRR (7)

Forward Neural Network (RepFNN) to serve as the Non-TFI
baseline. Next, we replaced the main architecture with a
CNN network (RepCNN) and an LSTM network (RepLSTM),
respectively. We designed comparative experiments for the
three modes on scenario d2. Please refer to Section H of [17]
for detailed results.
As shown in Fig. 12, The F1 of RepFNN decreases
by 5.75%-19.10% on average, while its RMSE values
(0.2687-0.2977) are signiﬁcantly higher than other variants,
demonstrating that the self-attention mechanism provides com-
plementary patterns beneﬁcial for both classiﬁcation and
imputation tasks. Similarly, the respective decrease of 1.55%-
9.40% and 1.91%-5.72% on F1 for RepCNN and RepLSTM
aligns with their inferior RMSE performance (RepCNN:
0.1785-0.1991; RepLSTM: 0.1605-0.1834) compared to the
complete TFI model (0.1524-0.1770). These consistent results
across both metrics conﬁrm that self-supervised tasks enhance
the Transformer encoder structure’s ability to learn packet
side-channel feature relationships, improving both classiﬁca-
tion accuracy and imputation precision.
D. Performance Analysis of N ¨uwa
1) Analysis of Time Complexity: We analyzed the perfor-
mance of N ¨uwa across 4 scenarios, focusing on two aspects:
the total number of model parameters and the prediction time
cost. The experimental results are shown in Table VI.First,
regarding model training parameters, the total number of
parameters across the 4 scenarios remains largely consistent,
with minor differences attributed to variations in vocabulary
sizes. Analysis of sequence length distributions reveals that in
scenariod2, approximately 1/3 of sessions exceed 512 tokens,
whereas this proportion is signiﬁcantly smaller in the other
three scenarios. Consequently, in d2, a higher proportion of
effective data packets are lost under the same packet loss
ratio, requiring more content to be imputed, which slightly
slows prediction speed. In contrast, scenariod4 has the highest
proportion of sequences under 512 tokens, necessitating the
least imputation and achieving the fastest prediction speed.
For detailed results, please refer to Fig. 15 in Section H.
2) Analysis of Trade-Offs Between Performance and Cost:
To analyze the trade-offs between model performance and
computational cost, we evaluated RMSE and prediction speed
across different layer conﬁgurations under various packet loss
rates. As shown in Fig. 13, deeper networks generally improve
RMSE but at the cost of higher computational demands. For
example, the 3-layer model, with 23.75M parameters, achieves
an average RMSE of 0.1972 and a prediction speed of 91
samples/s. In contrast, the 24-layer model reaches 172.56M
parameters, but its RMSE worsens to 0.2182 and its speed
drops to 16 samples/s, indicating overﬁtting risks.Among all
conﬁgurations, the 12-layer structure achieves a favorable
balance between performance and efﬁciency. It achieves the
lowest overall RMSE (0.1874) across all packet loss rates,
while maintaining a reasonable prediction speed of 28 sam-
ples/s. This makes it well-suited for applications that require
both high precision and moderate efﬁciency. Meanwhile, the
6-layer model performs slightly worse in terms of RMSE
(0.1897) but offers higher efﬁciency (58 samples per second),
making it a viable alternative for scenarios with stricter latency
constraints or limited computational resources.
In practical applications, the choice of model conﬁgura-
tion depends on the speciﬁc requirements of accuracy and
efﬁciency. For real-time or resource-constrained scenarios, a
lighter conﬁguration (3 or 6 layers) may sufﬁce. However,
for tasks that prioritize accuracy, the 12-layer model stands
out as an optimal choice, offering a solid trade-off between
computational cost and predictive performance. The 172M
model, combined with more diverse data to prevent overﬁtting,
is better suited for cloud-based environments with abundant
computational power.
VI. D ISCUSSION
In this section, we discuss the ﬁndings and limitations of
our work, as well as its potential to stimulate further research
in the ﬁeld.
A. Considerations of Application Domain and Limitation
The N¨uwa framework imputes missing trafﬁc data to reduce
training bias and improve prediction quality. We eschew pre-
training due to its impractical labeled data requirements,
proposing instead that combining our TFI component with
meta-learning or few-shot learning [25] can signiﬁcantly
improve encrypted trafﬁc classiﬁcation. Future work will
expand N ¨uwa’s current time-series imputation to include sta-
tistical features and raw data.
N¨uwa necessitates substantial computational resources for
training and ﬁne-tuning, with its computational parameter
count determined by the dimensionality d = 768, the number
of layers L = 12 , the maximum relative position k = 512 ,
and three sets of additional parameters Wq,r , Wk,r∈Rd×d,
as well as P∈R2k×d. The total number of parameters in the
N¨uwa framework, denoted as N = 2L×d2 + 2k×d = 88M.
This could limit its applicability in resource-constrained envi-
ronments. Compared to large models with parameter counts
in the hundreds of millions (B) to trillions (T), such as
community-developed pre-trained models (7B, 13B) [7], [26],
[27] and the GPT-4, LLaMA [28], [29] series (50B+), N ¨uwa’s
computational requirements are orders of magnitude smaller,
offering substantial application potential.
B. Theoretical Performance Analysis
To theoretically analyze the performance of the pre-trained
feature imputation model based on the Transformer encoder
and TFM strategy, several aspects need to be considered:

TFM Strategy. N¨uwa uses a sophisticated masking strat-

effectively restore missing raw data in trafﬁc, even in source-

training process is ﬂexible and can be conﬁgured according to
the network conditions. For networks with high randomness,
the Token mask ratio can be appropriately increased. In cases
of severe network congestion, increasing the Span Mask ratio
can enhance N ¨uwa’s ability to handle consecutive packet loss.
Computational Efﬁciency. Section V-B.3 analysis reveals
ZeroPad’s effectiveness in practical applications. By padding
packet loss locations with zeros, ZeroPad mitigates detection
performance degradation with minimal computational costs.
Its effectiveness stems from maintaining constant sequence
length and ﬁxed packet positions while ﬁltering noise. Con-
versely, the ﬁrst-order Markov model suffers from local
information constraints, inability to learn from extended
sequences, high resource demands, and limited imputation
capability with large trafﬁc volumes. For real-time trafﬁc
detection, ZeroPad provides an efﬁcient, low-cost solution to
counter packet loss effects on detection models.
Compound Parameter Changes. As TFI is built on the
Transformer Encoder architecture, it effectively mitigates the
long-distance forgetting problem, making it suitable for longer
sequences. While a 15% mask ratio may not always be optimal
for very long sequences, existing SOTA research [26] in NLP
suggests that the mask ratio can be slightly adjusted around
15% for long text/sequence inputs. Therefore, considering the
pre-training computational cost and prediction performance, a
15% mask ratio remains a stable choice in most scenarios.
Similarly, for longer input sequences, the appropriate mask
ratio and masking strategy can be adjusted according to the
speciﬁc TFM application scenarios to better align N ¨uwa with
practical use cases.
C. Considerations of UDP Protocol and Data Augmentation
Application in UDP. Given the prevalence of UDP in
real networks, we conducted complementary experiments on
UDP trafﬁc to better characterize packet loss in mixed trafﬁc
environments. However, due to UDP’s lack of a connection-
oriented transmission mechanism like TCP, existing methods

is notable. Our study primarily targets the restoration of side-

VII. R ELATED WORK
A. Trafﬁc Packet Disturbance
Trafﬁc packet perturbations in data center networks result
from congestion and blackhole incidents, manifesting as
packet loss, delays, and sequencing issues that impact appli-
cation performance. Fig. 1 identiﬁes three primary causes
of trafﬁc packet incompleteness: i) Complex topology -
intricate networks with sophisticated routing create trafﬁc
bursts through multiple link aggregation and excessive node
hops, causing link switching and packet loss; ii) Limited
probe - computational constraints of trafﬁc sniffers in high-
performance networks lead to partial packet capture and
missing session-level features; iii) Unpredictable network
properties - diverse application protocols, equipment age vari-
ations, congestion, weak signals, DoS attacks, and low voltage
conditions contribute to trafﬁc data loss.
From a side-channel feature perspective, we posit that
delayed packets exhibit out-of-order behavior in a time series.
Prior works, such as those by Tan et al. [9], have focused on
reducing packet loss through protocol optimization, developing
systems to detect and locate lost packets. Li et al. intro-
duced LossRadar [30], which captures detailed, ﬁne-grained
information on individual lost packets across networks. And
Rosetta [31] conducted 1%-10% packet loss during multi-
scenario trafﬁc replay to study the differences in heterogeneous
networks. While some research has aimed to locate missing

B. Feature Engineering and Imputation
Feature engineering-based NTAC typically preprocesses raw
data through steps such as deduplication and denoising, fol-
lowed by feature extraction and representation (e.g., data
augmentation) [3], [33]. The absence of certain side-channel
features from encrypted trafﬁc can result in sample devia-
tion, thereby impacting downstream classiﬁcation tasks. To
address this issue, many studies adopt a “Model+” approach
to preprocess input data, such as Xie et al introducing the
Rosetta model to enhance trafﬁc characterization and detection
model classiﬁcation as“Model + Rosetta”. We categorize the
impact of packet loss on side-channel features into three
types, including statistical features (e.g., expectations, stan-
dard deviations, averages, minima, maxima, alongside packet
counts and sizes, or inter-packet intervals) [34], [35], time
series features (e.g., sequences of packet sizes, directions, and
timestamps) [36], [37], and raw trafﬁc representations (which
involve encoding the raw trafﬁc into graphical or pictorial
forms) [38], [39], [40].
Statistical feature based Methods. Machine learning is
extensively applied in NATC [3], with scholars achieving
effective detection through statistical trafﬁc features across
time and frequency domains, complemented by machine learn-
ing. A seminal work, FlowPrint [35], introduced by Thijs et al.,
employs a semi-supervised approach to ﬁngerprint mobile
apps from network trafﬁc, encrypted or otherwise, enhancing
APP feature representation. Fu et al. [34] leveraged frequency
domain features to represent sequential information, aiming to
minimize information loss and bolster the detection model’s
robustness. Research [41] explores AI training trafﬁc gen-
eration using packet payloads and statistical features. While
conventional imputation methods like zero-padding, nearest-
neighbor ﬁlling, and mean imputation are common, they lack
contextual information and fail to capture temporal variations
in missing data, limiting performance improvements.
Time Series based Methods. Side-channel features of
network trafﬁc are inherently temporal, a focus of numerous
scholars [42]. Notably Zhao et al. [16] replayed network trafﬁc
and used DPDK and packet capture tools on both sender and
receiver sides to analyze the impact of packet loss. Addition-
ally, they simulated various potential scenarios using a random
packet loss strategy and proposed ERNN [16] to mitigate
trafﬁc packet perturbations. TSCRNN processes raw trafﬁc
via ﬂow segmentation and sampling, employing strategies to
sample midsections of long ﬂows for classiﬁcation. These
efforts aim to focus on longer ﬂows (more than 512) [1], [11]
for encrypted trafﬁc classiﬁcation. Additionally, Lin et al. [43]
introduced PEAN, a multi-modal DL framework utilizing raw
byte and length sequences with a self-attention mechanism
to discern relationships between network packets in bi-ﬂow.
Methods for imputing missing time series features, such as
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, introduce tem-
poral dependencies to enhance prediction accuracy. However,
they still face challenges when dealing with long intervals of
missing data.
Raw trafﬁc representations. Data augmentation is preva-
lent in NTAC, enhancing training data diversity by transform-
ing trafﬁc data into images, graphs, or sentences, namely,
raw trafﬁc representations [33]. For instance, FlowPic [39]
facilitates encrypted trafﬁc classiﬁcation and application iden-
tiﬁcation by converting ﬂow sequence data into images for
CNN-based identiﬁcation. Similarly, Kim et al. [44] and
ProGraph [40] represents another approach, fusing multiple
trafﬁc features into graphs using IP nodes or ports, enhancing
trafﬁc classiﬁcation with graph neural networks. Additionally,
scholars like Li et al. [45] enhance data diversity and defend
against analyzers using online trafﬁc perturbation with Prism.
Detection methods based on representations rely on the raw
trafﬁc data. While imputation techniques, such as SAGCRN
[6], proposed a trafﬁc data completion model based on graph
convolutional network model to impute missing values. This
model utilizes graph convolution to model the local spatial
dependency. This work shares similarities with our approach,
but it is less effective than Transformer models in handling
long-distance dependencies and global information. Addition-
ally, the graph neural network (GNN) approach requires a
substantial number of labeled samples, whereas pre-training
methods do not necessitate labeling. They still face signiﬁcant
limitations when dealing with large-scale, high-dimensional,
and highly nonlinear trafﬁc data.
C. Pre-Training Models
As previously noted, self-supervised learning is increasingly
utilized for ﬂow representation. Recently, Transformer-based
pre-trained models have emerged as highly effective neural
network architectures for language modeling [46]. Examples
include BERT [7], RoBERTa [18], GPT [28], and BART [27].
In NATC, variants like ET-BERT [20] and PERT [47] build
upon the BERT. ET-BERT pre-trains a trafﬁc representation
model on large-scale unlabeled data to produce deep contextu-
alized datagram-level representations, ﬁne-tunable with limited
labeled data. PERT employs a transformer model for auto-
matic trafﬁc feature extraction using advanced dynamic word
embeddings. Additionally, transformer encoders encode multi-
modal features for ﬂow representation fusion [48]. Zhao et al.
[8] introduced a Masked AutoEncoder (MAE) based trafﬁc
transformer with multi-level ﬂow representation. ET-BERT
capitalizes on open-domain unlabeled trafﬁc data to learn
generalized representations, similar to leveraging pre-trained
outputs for downstream classiﬁcation tasks.
Research related to our work falls into two categories:
regular classiﬁcation without missing data imputation and
feature imputation across various scenarios. Our approach
differs in three key ways: (i) we impute missing trafﬁc side-
channel features before applying classiﬁcation, rather than
performing direct classiﬁcation; (ii) unlike existing feature
imputation methods focused on domains like smart trans-
portation, our work speciﬁcally addresses network trafﬁc
imputation; (iii) N ¨uwa framework overcomes challenges in
simulating network packet loss while reducing labeling costs
through unsupervised self-training.

VIII. C ONCLUSION
In this paper, we present a comprehensive study span-
ning diverse simulated and real-world Internet scenarios,
demonstrating that existing network trafﬁc analyzers experi-
ence signiﬁcant performance degradation when dealing with
fragmented side-channel features. To tackle the issues, we
introduce the N ¨uwa framework, which employs a pre-trained
TFI to learn contextual trafﬁc side-channel features through
TFM strategy. Experimental results on four practical scenarios
demonstrate the effectiveness and generalization of our pro-
posed methods, which promises superior performance to the
state-of-the-art methods. Extensive experiments show that TFI
robustly recovers missing side-channel features across various
network conditions, with imputed features aiding in enhancing
the performance of current DL models.
